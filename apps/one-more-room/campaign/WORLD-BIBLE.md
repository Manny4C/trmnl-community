# The Lantern Marches — world bible

Established foundation · October 28, 2026. This document develops the existing stolen-sun premise; it does not claim that any individual party has already completed the new chapters.

## The promise of the world

The sun was taken out of the sky, but not all light disappeared. Small embers remain in old places where people once made promises to one another. Lamps still burn. Seeds can still wake. A bell can still tell someone they are not alone.

The Lantern Company carries the last freely traveling lantern. Its four companions search the underworld for embers held by ancient keepers. Returning enough light is the long quest. The nearer question is how to use the small amount they have without forgetting the people who kept it alive.

The world is worn, inhabited and repairable. It is not empty ruins with a monster in every room. People maintain bridges, ration oil, trade seeds, preserve names and disagree about which work should come first. The dark is dangerous; it is not evidence that kindness was foolish.

## Fixed laws

1. **Light is carried, not created from nothing.** Ordinary fire, the company lantern and sun embers differ in strength, but none is a limitless solution. The existing engine abstracts supplies and recovery; do not invent a second fuel meter without designing it.
2. **An ember holds an obligation as well as power.** The keepers were entrusted with light. Some remember their purpose better than others. Defeating a keeper can free its ember without proving that every keeper was evil.
3. **The lantern brings its bearers back from defeat.** This is the game's established recovery, with a loss of gold. It is not universal resurrection. Do not use it to revive an unrelated dead NPC or undo a costly promise.
4. **Names preserve identity, not omniscience.** Learning a name can connect records or help someone remember. It does not grant mind reading, teleportation or command over the named person.
5. **The lost sun is recoverable, but the full explanation is not yet established.** Early sources disagree about why it was taken. Preserve that uncertainty. Do not casually reveal a final culprit, destroy the sun permanently or replace the central quest.
6. **Time in an expedition is local.** A browser action, a TRMNL update and a real-world Monday are not equal lengths of in-world time. Weekly writing must not age every party by seven days.

## The company

### Brann — guardian

Brann notices who is standing nearest a danger and usually puts himself there. He trusts useful work more than speeches. His humor is dry and brief. He can hold a passage, carry weight and make a plain promise; he cannot force agreement by being honorable.

Private pressure: he fears that protecting everyone means making every decision for them. Write moments in which he learns to ask for help. Do not invent a tragic lost family merely to motivate a scene; no such history has been established.

Voice: “One at a time. I'll hold it.”

### Pip — rogue

Pip notices the missing hinge, the loose coin and the person pretending not to be frightened. They like getting the better of an obstacle, not automatically getting the better of every friend. Their jokes release tension; they should not sabotage a heartfelt scene.

Private pressure: accepting a gift without treating it as a debt. Let generosity sometimes surprise them. Cleverness has limits; a concealed mechanism still needs a real way to be found or disarmed.

Voice: “That's a very expensive way to lock a cupboard.”

### Mira — mage

Mira studies marks, patterns and the costs hidden inside old rules. She can misunderstand an inscription. Her magic clarifies or protects; it should not become a new power every time the author needs an exit.

Private pressure: wanting a complete explanation before taking an imperfect but useful action. Give her discoveries that help someone now without answering the whole mystery.

Voice: “I can read enough. It says not to pull twice.”

### Fern — ranger

Fern notices water, weather, living things and the route back. She is practical, patient and capable of changing her mind when the ground proves her wrong. Her knowledge comes from tracks and experience, not from automatic access to every forest's memory.

Private pressure: whether tending one small living thing is worth slowing the larger quest. The campaign's answer should emerge through consequences rather than a sermon.

Voice: “Something still grows here. Step around it.”

## Places already on the atlas

The six names below are established dungeon families. Each generated floor can be another connected site within that family. Avoid claiming that a defeated unique keeper has regenerated in the identical chamber.

### The Sunken Abbey

Water sits above doorways and drips upward into cracks. Old bells were once used to coordinate people across flooded passages. The abbey's central duty was to keep safe routes known. Its failing bell network makes repair, guidance and rescue natural stories.

Useful questions: who maintained the ropes, which bell warns of rising water, who is still waiting to be heard? A bell's sound can travel along a connected passage or messenger route; it cannot conveniently cross the entire world.

The Bellkeeper is an inherited role among sun-bound guardians. An encounter with one is not proof that all bells or all members of the abbey are hostile.

### The Briar Archive

Roots hold shelves together and push through records left by people who feared forgetting. Some books are alive in a limited sense: they grow, respond to light or preserve marks. They do not contain every fact the writer wants to disclose.

Useful questions: which record is worth carrying, what must be repaired before a shelf is opened, who has mistaken a copy for an original? Protecting a living book can conflict with removing an ember; give that conflict a practical solution instead of a forced moral trap.

Thorn Wardens were charged with keeping knowledge from being stripped without care. A particular warden can become an obstacle without making the archive itself an enemy.

### The Clockwork Crypt

Brass mechanisms still carry out work whose instructions have become incomplete. Bells, gates and clocks can be repaired with tools and a readable cause. Machines do not become magical wish engines when repaired.

Useful questions: what task was the machine meant to perform, which missing piece matters, whose convenience is the device costing someone else? A repaired water pump or safe timing mechanism is a satisfying payoff on its own.

Brass Sentinels protect entrusted mechanisms. Releasing one from a corrupted order may require understanding that order; the engine still supports a simple fight where needed.

### The Glass Catacombs

Reflections preserve fragments, not perfect recordings. A mirror can show an old light or a damaged image, but cannot prove a person's motives. Crossing a reflected threshold needs a known physical entrance; avoid surprise world travel through any shiny object.

Useful questions: which reflection is current, how can a message be checked, what can be learned without breaking a preserved image? Glass Widows is the traditional name for the keepers of abandoned reflection halls, not a blanket declaration that the next opponent is a giant spider.

### The Ember Observatory

The instruments were built to study the sky when there was a sky to study. Ash, star charts and calibration marks connect the lost sun to ordinary patient work. Predictions should have a source and a margin of uncertainty.

Useful questions: what can an old chart still predict, which light is a signal rather than a star, who is using a false forecast to gain support? The Ash Astronomer is a keeper's office; one defeated office-holder does not make every astronomer a villain.

### The Hollow Orchard

Silver fruit hums quietly and old roots reach into sheltered chambers. The orchard survived by sharing stored warmth and seed. It cannot grow a complete harvest in one frame merely because the company was kind.

Useful questions: who gets a scarce seed, what can be repaired to protect new growth, what is a fair promise for a future harvest? Root Kings are bound keepers of particular old groves. An old root network can carry small signals; its reach must be established before it conveys information.

## People to return to

### Nessa Reed — ferrier and route keeper

Home: the wet approaches to the Sunken Abbey. Nessa maintains a practical ferry route, not a portal. She wants people and needed supplies to cross safely. She distrusts impressive claims made by someone unwilling to carry a repair plank.

She knows local currents, bell signals and the people she has actually ferried. She does not know every keeper's secret. Elsewhere, use a letter or a known courier rather than pretending Nessa arrived ahead of the company without cause.

Long thread: a bell once used to request the ferry can no longer be heard. Helping with its signal earns a real favor, not permanent free resources.

### Orin Vale — walking mender

Route: the maintained waystones linking the six site families. Orin is one of a small number of couriers who can carry messages along those routes. His travel is slow, costly and motivated. He may plausibly be encountered at a rest station in any family because that route is his work.

He wants a network that functions for ordinary people. He knows where he has been, what he has repaired and who sent a message. He does not know what an unopened message says. He will not become the all-purpose source of every new quest.

Long thread: why are repairs being marked complete when he can still see them broken? This is a future investigation, not yet proof of sabotage.

### Tamsin Quill — archive conservator

Home: the Briar Archive's outer reading room. Tamsin protects living records and makes careful copies for travelers. She can be stubborn about procedure because careless copying has harmed real people.

She wants records that can be used without being consumed. She knows her collection and its gaps; a missing page is an absence, not permission for an invented quotation. Her correspondence can travel with Orin.

Long thread: someone has removed the names of small communities from supply records. The author must establish who benefits before inventing an antagonist.

### Ada Flint — lampwright

Home: a workshop on the Clockwork Crypt's maintained edge. Ada repairs housings, shutters and simple mechanisms. She can make a lamp use its existing fuel more reliably; she cannot manufacture a sun ember.

She wants tools that can be maintained by the people who depend on them. She dislikes a “gift” that creates permanent dependence. A fair deal with her can create a durable relationship and a useful future option.

### Sel Marr — keeper of public notices

Home: the Glass Catacombs' entry hall. Sel compares reports and labels uncertain accounts as uncertain. Their job is not secret prophecy. They can help the party test a claim by locating an independent witness or record.

They want people to act on trustworthy warnings. Their weakness is postponing a warning until it is too late to be useful. A scene can let the party choose how to communicate uncertainty without forcing a lie.

### Ivo Moss — seed steward

Home: the Hollow Orchard's shelter. Ivo tends a small stock of surviving seeds and records who took responsibility for each. A promised harvest is not an immediate resource reward.

They want tomorrow to have living things in it. They will accept a repaired shelter, a carried message or a kept promise as help; not every quest must be paid for with gold.

## Factions and limits

### The Lantern Road

A loose network of ferriers, menders and lampwrights. Goal: keep ordinary routes usable. Resources: modest tools, messages, hospitality and practical trust. Limit: they cannot defend every road or command the keepers.

Tension: some members want to spend every available ember on the road now. Others support carrying the light farther. This disagreement can stay good-faith.

Initial clock: **Broken Signals**, 0/4. Proposed steps: missed message; delayed crossing; isolated rest station; coordinated repair effort. Do not advance it automatically in a party save each real-world week.

### The Briar Custodians

Conservators of records, seeds and living memory. Goal: preserve what a restored world will need. Resources: carefully kept stores, old knowledge and trained caretakers. Limit: collections have gaps; preservation is not a universal veto over helping people.

Tension: a record may need to travel to be useful, while travel can damage it. Let people invent reasonable safeguards.

Initial clock: **The Missing Names**, 0/4. The steps remain draft leads until evidence and causes are written into a released chapter. Do not establish a culprit in a status line.

### The Quiet Measure

Surveyors and instrument keepers who allocate scarce light using measurements. Goal: avoid spending the last useful resources on an untested promise. Resources: instruments, charts and influence over stores. Limit: their measurements can be incomplete and their authority is local.

Tension: caution can save a community or become an excuse to abandon it. Avoid making the whole faction secretly evil. A well-supported disagreement is more useful than another masked cult.

Initial clock: **A Forecast of Dawn**, 0/4. Its next legitimate development is someone presenting an uncertain measurement, not an actual sunrise or an immediate attack.

## The opening chapter: The Bell Without a Rope

This is an invitation into the world's ordinary work. A courier's message asks for a broken signal to be made useful. A marked waystone shows that the signal and a nearby route once worked together. The company restores enough of that route for someone else to use it and receives a human acknowledgment.

The required arc has four short beats: receive the message, test the signal, repair the way, and acknowledge the result. Each can be resolved with simple look/help/repair/talk choices. A setback still passes the clue along but can cost a ration, gold or a little health. The chapter ends with a kept signal and a useful connection, not the recovery of the whole sun.

The optional thread concerns an ordinary letter that did not arrive. Starting it commits the author to a reachable return or resolution. It can establish whether the company handled another person's words carefully. No party is assumed to have started it.

The exact per-frame scenes and effects live in the foundation runtime pack. The release dossier records why each one belongs and which canon it draws on.

## Secrets and future questions

The following are **draft possibilities**, not facts:

- The sun may have been divided to prevent one destructive use, rather than stolen for simple greed.
- A keeper's original oath may require returning light once neighboring duties are met.
- The missing names may be a record-keeping failure being exploited, a deliberate exclusion, or both.
- Someone may be predicting a dawn that will light only a small privileged part of the world.

Do not publish all four. Choose only when earlier clues support one, then mark rejected alternatives as discarded plans. The final campaign must leave room for the user's future direction.

## Writing reminders

Make the world understandable before making it mysterious. Return to a repaired bridge. Let a person recognize a favor. Show that a ration came from somebody's small store. Let an odd light be beautiful without requiring a fight. If a scene would still make sense with every proper name changed, strengthen its link to this world before adding it.
